That Urgent Email From Your Bank Is Probably Lying to You

Your bank would never email you at 11:47 pm to tell you your account will be closed in two hours unless you verify your details immediately. And yet, when that message lands in your inbox, complete with your bank's logo, your name spelled correctly, and a link that looks almost right, a small part of your brain still wonders: what if this one is real? That moment of doubt is exactly what phishing emails are designed to create. They do not need you to be careless. They need you to be busy, tired, or worried for about ten seconds. For small businesses, the stakes are even higher, because one clicked link can hand criminals the keys to your company bank account, your client data, and your email system in a single afternoon.

What phishing actually is

Phishing is a confidence trick delivered by email. The sender pretends to be someone you trust, usually your bank, HMRC, a delivery company, Microsoft, or a supplier you work with, and tries to get you to click a link, open an attachment, or hand over login details. The link takes you to a fake login page that looks identical to the real one. You type in your password, and the criminals now have it. The attachment installs malware that watches what you type or locks your files until you pay a ransom. The most dangerous phishing emails are the targeted ones, sometimes called spear phishing, where the attacker has done homework on your business, knows your suppliers, and writes an email that reads like it came from a real colleague. Those are much harder to spot, and they are increasingly common against small UK businesses.

The urgency trick

Almost every phishing email relies on urgency. Your account will be suspended. A payment failed. A parcel cannot be delivered. A legal notice requires action today. Urgency is the attacker's best friend because it switches off your careful thinking and pushes you toward acting first and checking later. Real banks and real government departments do not operate like this. HMRC will not threaten you with arrest by email. Your bank will not give you two hours to verify your identity or lose your account. If an email makes your stomach tighten and your first instinct is to click immediately, that feeling is your best warning sign. Stop, breathe, and check the message properly before you do anything.

Check the sender address, not the display name

The display name on an email is just a label, and anyone can set it to anything. An email that says "Barclays" in your inbox could have been sent from any address on the internet. Always look at the actual sender address. Real bank emails come from the bank's own domain, such as barclays.co.uk. A phishing email might come from barclays-secure-support.com, barclayscustomerservice.net, or a completely unrelated domain that has been made to look similar. Watch for tiny tricks: a zero instead of the letter O, a missing letter, or an extra word slipped into the domain. If the address looks even slightly off, do not click anything in the message. Go to the bank's website yourself, or call the number on the back of your card, and ask whether there is really a problem.

Hover before you click

On a computer, hover your mouse over any link in an email without clicking it, and your email programme will show you the real destination at the bottom of the screen. Phishing links almost always point somewhere different from what the text suggests. A button that says "Verify your account" might lead to a random domain registered yesterday. On a phone, press and hold the link to preview it. If the destination does not match the company the email claims to be from, delete the message. Never use the phone number printed in a suspicious email either, because that number may connect you to the criminals. Use the number on the company's official website or on your bank card.

The attachment trap

Unexpected attachments are one of the most reliable signs of a phishing attempt. Invoices, delivery notices, and "updated terms and conditions" are common disguises. A real supplier you work with regularly will usually send invoices through their normal system, not as a surprise attachment from an unfamiliar address. If you were not expecting an attachment, do not open it, even if the filename looks harmless. Modern malware often hides inside Word documents and PDFs that ask you to enable macros or click a button to view content. That prompt is the trap. If a supplier has genuinely sent you something, phone them on a number you already have and confirm it before opening the file.

Language clues that give the game away

Phishing emails used to be easy to spot because the English was terrible. AI writing tools have made them much more polished, but clues remain. Look for generic greetings like "Dear customer" when the company normally uses your name. Look for slightly odd phrasing, inconsistent formatting, or a footer that does not match the company's real emails. Compare the message with a genuine email from the same company. Does the logo look stretched or low quality? Is the signature different? Does the email ask for information the company already has? Your bank already knows your account number and sort code. It will never email you asking for your full password or your one-time passcode. Anyone who asks for those is a criminal, no matter how official the email looks.

Why small businesses are prime targets

Criminals target small businesses because they know the defences are usually thinner than at a large company. There is often no IT department, no dedicated security training, and one person handling finance, email, and everything else. A single employee clicking one bad link can give an attacker access to your email, which they can then use to send fake invoices to your clients or redirect payments to their own account. This type of fraud, called invoice fraud or payment diversion, costs UK small businesses millions every year. The phishing email is often just the first step. Once inside, the attacker watches your email quietly for weeks, learning how you invoice and who you pay, before striking at the moment that hurts most.

What to do when you spot one

If you receive a suspicious email, do not click anything, do not reply, and do not download any attachments. Report it using the free service provided by the National Cyber Security Centre: forward the email to report@phishing.gov.uk. Your email provider may also have its own report button. Then delete the message. If you clicked a link or entered details before realising, act fast. Change your password immediately from the real website, not from any link in the email. Turn on two-factor authentication. Contact your bank and tell them what happened. The sooner you report it, the better your chance of stopping any damage. There is no shame in being caught out. These emails are designed by professionals to fool busy people, and reporting them helps protect everyone else.

How to protect your business day to day

Good habits beat expensive software. Turn on two-factor authentication on every account that offers it, especially email and banking. Use a password manager so that every account has a unique, strong password, which means a stolen password from one site cannot unlock the rest of your business. Keep your software updated, because updates fix the security holes that malware exploits. Train everyone in your business, even if that is just you and one other person, to pause before clicking links in unexpected emails. A simple rule helps: if an email asks you to log in, pay, or download something, close the email and go to the service directly through your browser or app. That one habit defeats most phishing attempts on its own. The bottom line

Phishing works because it exploits trust and urgency, not because its victims are foolish. The criminals only need you to be rushed once. You need to be careful every time, which sounds unfair, but the habits that protect you are simple: check the sender, hover over links, never trust urgency, and always go to the real website yourself. Teach those habits to everyone who touches your business email, report every attempt you spot, and keep your defences switched on. A few seconds of caution before you click is the cheapest security your business will ever buy.
